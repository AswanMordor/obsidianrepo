# History of Baji Quan
## Wu Zhong and Baji Quan 
* Origins are unclear; however, most accounts state that Wu Zhong was the founder in the early-mid 1700s, localizing in Cangxian (Cang County Prefecture) (**3**) or Mengcun Hui Autonomous County, in Hebei Province (**4**). Wu Zhong was more renowned for the spear than open-handed martial arts, and was likely recognized by the Shaolin Temple as one of the best spear masters of his time (**3**).
* This is due to martial arts generally being oral traditions in local communities and lineages (where family lineages dominated). Especially notable is that many records were lost during the Cultural Revolution, which muddles history especially regarding martial arts considering they were few reliable records to begin with.
* What is known is that Baji Quan quickly became popular with militaries and bodyguards due to its simple principles and movements (relative to its cousins that contained many flourishes and a higher barrier or entry) and practical approach to unwanted conflict in closer quarters.
## Meaning of Baji Quan and Philosophy
- The "Ba" in Baji is commonly interpreted in two ways. Firstly, the eight unarmed weapons: "head shoulders, elbows, buttocks, hips, knees, and feet" (**1**). Secondly, the eight directions. (For further context from the interview with Master (NAME) **2**) These eight directions refer to the eight compass directions, however, the interpretation is *all* directions; stating that Baji Quan can respond to threats in any direction.
- The "Ji" in Baji is commonly interpreted as "extremes". When placed with the first interpretation of "Ba", body parts, Baji can mean the extremes of unarmed combat. However, it is much more commonly interpreted as the eight extremes, meaning the ability to react in all directions with the highest capability. Of note, Baji Quan is not considered a longer-range martial art even with the "extreme of eight directions" interpretation. It is commonly paired with a complementary style called PiGuaZhang, with the phrase "When Baji and PiGuaZhang are combined..." (**2**)

## Guiding Approaches and Principles
Like many variations of Kung Fu, the idea of a push-pull is expressed as a closing and opening of the upper or whole body. In Baji Quan, many whole-body techniques are practiced as closing the distance with the body closed and coiled like a spring, then released with the striking arm moving forward and the rear-arm pulling back, opening up the chest and delivering explosive power as the body is opened.
Baji Quan also practices lower stances, with ultra-low (90 degree bend or lower) stances reserved solely for strength and balance training; however, a 30-45 degree bend is fairly common, especially when executing whole body movement. This makes sense during the more ramming-style movements which are common throughout Baji Quan flavors.

Per (**1**), the six major techniques of Baji Quan involve:
Ding (顶): Headbutt or pressing forward/upward with the head. Bao (抱): Embrace or wrapping motion with the arms.  
Ti (提): Lifting or raising motion.  
Kua (挎): Striking/swinging with the hip or shoulder.
Chan (缠): Entwining or coiling/twisting motion.  
Dan (单): A technique often interpreted as a single-handed strike or movement, emphasizing simplicity and directness
### Close Range
- Baji Quan is a close-range martial art, relying on footwork to close the distance quickly and strike from within the pocket/clinch distance.
### Linearity - Pushing Forward with the Whole Body
- "Fa Jing" via the use of the whole-body with the hips, waist, and footwork to deliver quick strong strikes. (**1**)
### Continuous Strong Attacks
- The strikes of Baji Quan in contrast to a style like Wing Chun are strong and quick but generally not delivered in a barrage, with each strike deliberate and varied in targeting. (**1**)

## The Push
* This can be done with a variety of upper body strikes including neck strikes, wrapping the arm around the body, etc. but is more commonly done with elbows. Interestingly the elbows are either coming down around the clavicle, up around the midsection, or straight across to the midsection. Elbows to the head as strikes are less common as Baji Quan prefers to get the opponent to the ground whether by throw, trip, or joint control. The essence of the push stems from moving the whole body via the hips rather than the upper body, and placing the lead leg far into the stance of the opponent. Therefore, when the strike is delivered, the lead leg acts as a lever to trip or reap the opponent down.

## Blocking and Attacking
- Similar in nature to Master Fitzgerald's seminar. Baji Quan approaches blocking as a close-range softer deflection than a hard block. The initial blocking motion is generally via the diagonally-opposing hand and is quickly (near simultaneously) followed-up by a quick strike the the same-side arm. The deflecting hand often grabs the incoming strike once deflected, giving opportunity for a striking target and/or joint lock to occur. (See images)

## The Groin Strike
- Swinging the arm in an arc downward towards the groin, using a cupping-hand strike the groin with the palm-heel and above
## The Foot Trap
Notice the upon advancing, as an alternative to stepping deep into the opponent's stance, the lead foot was stepped on to allow for a better probability of the uppercut landing. Since Baji Quan relies heavily on advancing and keeping initiative, this is one way to prevent the opponent from retreating away and resetting. 
## The Shoulder Check
Can be performed with only rotational motion or by lateral foot work. If done with lateral motion, this is usually combined with rotational motion via moving the back leg through to the opposite stance during the shoulder check motion.
## The Neck Grab and Throw
- Basically just run them down after wrapping the arm around the neck
- No need for trips or throws per se...just slip inside the defense, wrap the arm as you fast walk forward; can use the other arm as support; possibly try to get a leg behind their leg (coming from the side to the back) as quickly as possible in case they backstep
