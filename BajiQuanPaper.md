## Introduction

Bajiquan (八極拳), "eight extremes", is a style of Chinese Kungfu containing close-quarters striking, tripping,  throwing, and the practice of Chinese longspear. Unarmed Bajiquan has been widely used by Chinese and Taiwanese militaries and police forces, as well as bodyguarding: notably the Taiqanese presidential guard for its simplicity, directness, and aim of neutralizing opponents by tripping, pushing, or throwing them to the ground. Bajiquan containes no flourishes seen in more theatrical kungfu styles, and relies on a solid lower body structure with short direct strikes to lead into an eventual takedown. 

In this paper, I will give a brief account of Bajiquan's history, guiding principles, and my personal favorite techniques that embody Bajiquan's unique approach to Chinese martial arts.

## History

Like many Chinese kungfu styles, the origins of Bajiquan are unclear, being shrouded in quasi-legend with known historical figues mixed with hazy oral retellings. Some of this uncertainty lies in the dominance of oral histories throughout Chinese martial arts lineages; however, some theorize that many written records were destroyed during the Cultural Revolution (1966 to 1976). Whatever the reason, the earliest Bajiquan practitioner confirmed by written record was Wu Zhong, a matrial artist in the early-mid 1700s who is commonly called the founder of Bajiquan. A commonly told legend states that Wu Zhong learned Bajiquan from a wandering monk in the Cang County Prefecture or Hebei Province. This monk passed down his unarmed techniques to Wu Zhong, who combined them with local styles and his own longspear art, forming Bajiquan as we know it. Other accounts state that Bajiquan has existed for much longer than the 1700s through the Hui people of Cang County Prefecture, however, the consildation and naming of Bajiquan was only done in Wu Zhong's time. What is known is that Wu Zhong was a skilled spear master and passed down his skills to multiple people, eventually forming a number of lineages carrying on Bajiqun within their families. Some of these families fled to Taiwan, where Bajiquan has continued to thrive in schools, police forces, and military bodyguards.

The "Ba" in Baji is commonly intrepreted in two ways. Firstly, the eight unarmed weapons: the head, shoulders, elbows, buttocks, hips, knees, and feet. Secondly, the eight directions. These eight directions refer to the eight common compass directions, however, as traditional Chinese phrases are apt to do, the eight directions are used as a metaphor. The implied meaning: Bajiquan is capable of responding to threats in any and all directions.

The "Ji" in Baji is commonly interpreted to mean "extremes". Combined with the first intepretation of "Ba", Bajiquan could mean "the extremes of unarmed combat" alluding to Bajiquan's aggressive nature. However, most Bajiquan sources use the second intrepretation of "Ba", yeilding the "eight extremes". This more common intepretation states that Bajiquan has the ability to react in any direction with the utmost capability.

## Guiding Approaches and Principles

Bajiquan's main purpose is to close distance with the opponent, usually superimposing one leg with the opponent's stance, and initiate a takedown. Some substyles of Bajiquan contain further groundwork, while others stop at takedown and subsequent strike. Bajiquan contains no obvious blocks, instead relying on deflections and grabs. In this way, Bajiquan is all aggression as every deflection is paired with a near simultaneous strike and closing footwork. Footwork and stance structure comprise the essence of Bajiquan, as many instructors assert a stable stance and body structure as the fundimental means of power generation. Nearly all Bajiquan attacks consist of footwork that steps into an opponent's stance, and whole body ramming attacks that either push the opponent away or take them down. Trips and reaps are staples, while Judo-esq throws are absent.

### Stance
Bajiquan has many of the common stances seen throughout Chinese kungfu including Mǎbù (馬步) "Horse Stance", Gōng Bù (弓步) "Bow Stance", and Gai Bu (盖步) "Cross Step/Stance". Bajiquan keeps its stances low compared to karate, and emphasizes wider legs with firmly planted feet. Rather than coiled like a spring for explosive lunges or kicks, Bajiquan's stances serve as a stable base to undermine the opponents stance and make it easier to initiate trips, reaps, and other takedowns. A useful way to envision the utility of Bajiquan stancework is to picture the low stance as the base for a battering ram, where Bajiquan footwork simply rams the user into the opponent's space with full body weight, lower center of gravity, and a sturdier means of absorbing recoil. Bajiquan practicioners train their stances around 90 degrees or lower, while staying in stance positons such as the one below for minutes at times before very slowly transitioning to the next stance.

During these slow stance drills the upper body remains relatively relaxed with many schools choosing to incorporate upper body techniques in conjunction with stance transitions, similiar to standard Chinese forms or kata. While some schools choose to have students practice explosive power in upper body techniques such as elbows and hammer fists, the instructors that I found to be the most practical all drilled upper body techniques in a relaxed fashion while the main priority was building structure through stancework. In my research, full-speed stancework was likened to fast walking in stance rather than lunging or jumping, and in contrast to slow stancework, was mainly drilled with partners. 

Below are more detailed explinations of the three main stances found in Bajiquan: Mǎbù (馬步) "Horse Stance", Gōng Bù (弓步) "Bow Stance", and Gai Bu (盖步) "Cross Step/Stance".

**Mǎbù (馬步) "Horse Stance"**
![[Pasted image 20250323105302.png]]
(ref: https://yanchenwuguan.com/basics-stances-in-shaolin-kungfu/)

Mabu is named "horse stance" as one can imagine a horse being ridden. This stance is most similiar to Kiba Dachi with the main difference being an increased width and depth. The toes are traditionally pointed forward, however, many Bajiquan schools are more relaxed with toe position than other traditional kungfu styles or morden Wushu. Knees are actively pushed apart so as not to  cave inwards, and the upper body remains upright at the hips.

**Gōng Bù (弓步) "Bow Stance"**
![[Pasted image 20250323110327.png]]
(ref: https://yanchenwuguan.com/basics-stances-in-shaolin-kungfu/)

Gong Bu is named "bow stance" as one can imagine drawing back a large bow, using the forward leaning stance to create a stable base. Gong Bu is most similiar to Zenkutsu Dachi with the main difference being the increased depth of Gong Bu, putting more weight on the front leg as compared to Zenkutsu Dachi. Gong Bu is traditionally performed with close to an 80-90 degree bend in the front leg while keeping the back leg straight. Depening on the style and technique, the upper body can remain upright at the hips, or fall in line with the angle of the back leg.

**Gai Bu (盖步) "Cross Step/Stance"**
![[GaiBu.png]]
(ref: https://youtu.be/1eDDyXoUi_s?feature=shared, "This Bajiquan attack is DANGEROUS, Kevin Lee")

The Gai Bu stance in Chinese kungfu varies from a fully kneeling position to a cross step as shown above. Many kungfu styles including modern Wushu and Taichi use a much longer and lower cross step; however, Bajiquan utilizes a relatively narrow cross step transition for setting up the rear leg to move inside or around the opponent's stance. While this stance is unstable from the side, it provides decent stability in the direction of motion while allowing the user to reposition the back leg without breaking the walking rhythm. This is useful as movement in Bajiquan tends to remain at one pace so as to remain stable throughout.

## Upper Body Techniques

Bajiquan has six foundational techniques listed below. While I've categorized these into upper body techniques, nearly all of these awould be paired with footwork to move the whole body towards the opponent during technique execution.

**Ding (顶)**: Thrusting with the elbow. Bajiquan uses elbow strikes as ramming thrusts rather than rotational strikes, uppercuts, or dowards hits.
![[Pasted image 20250323122109.png]]

**Bao (抱)**: Embracing. These motions use the inner side forearm when the palm faces upwards (what we would normally consider the outer forearm).
![[Pasted image 20250323122701.png]]

**Dan (单)**: Bouncing. A single-handed strike with the top of the forearm which can be thought of as a verticle open-handed backfist, but with the striking object being the forearm. Instead of a "snapping" motion, this forearm strike bounces like a spring, knocking against the target and using the recoil to return to the initial loaded position.
![[Pasted image 20250323122816.png]]

**Ti (提)**: Raising. An uppercut with a fist; contrary to the Isshinryu uppercut this technique comes vertically up with the wrist in-line with the forearm. The arm moves up at the shoulder as the upper body expands at the chest to add more mass to the movement.
![[Pasted image 20250323123259.png]]

**Kua (挎)**: Rotation at the hips. Hip rotation is not normally used for strikes; however, it is implemented when in takedown range of an opponenet. The movement demonstrated below can be used as a type of hammer fist strike, but in my research was much more widely used as a means of throwing the opponent while one leg was used as a lever either between or on the outside of the opponent's legs.
![[Pasted image 20250323124039.png]]
![[Pasted image 20250323124143.png]]
![[Pasted image 20250323124202.png]]

**Chan (缠)**: Twining. Twining is a combination of parrying and the kungfu concept of "sticky hands". The inner side of the forearm is used to keep contact with the opponent's arm as the user's arm moves in a circular motion at the elbow. The key is to keep contact until the opponet's arm can be manipulated either by grab, lock, or providing an opportunity for an attack/throw. I have not seen a good example of this parry being used to outright deflect or strike the arm. Often the utility of twining is as a means to gain control of an arm rather than knock it away.

## Ding - The Quintessential Bajiquan Strike
![[Pasted image 20250323103637.png]]

The above image, shown again, represents the quintessential Bajiquan elbow strike. Consistent with the fundamental technique of Ding, the Bajiquan elbow is not a result of rotational motion. The strike is a thrust or ram attack, where the elbow is kept in-line with the front knee as the whole body closes distance into the opponent's space. As Bajiquan contains a longspear compnent, it is apt to consider the elbow as the tip of a spear thrust, as the rest of the body is the shaft. Therefore, the elbow and body move as one unit at the same speed. The result is an elbow-tipped ram attack into the midsection/sternum area, with a low center of gravity, full weight, and full committment. 

The principles presented in the traditional Bajiquan elbow provide a general framewok for how Bajiquan operates as a whole. Though containing a variety of ramming strikes and takedowns, Bajiquan uses similiar movement patterns and principles for nearly every approach.

The steps to execute this technique are simple:
1. Move forward into the opponent's space while dropping the center of gravity.
2. As the front leg steps into range, grind the front foot into the ground and twist the front leg: yeilding the transition to Mabu (horse stance). If in the process the front knee hits against the opponent's leg to destabilize them, even better. Simultaneously form the elbow just before impact. Use the back arm as a counterweight to the front arm.
3. Ram into the opponent with the elbow perpendicular to the target area, while dropping the center of gravity slightly futher.

The below images show the transition into the ramming elbow strike. Note that the Bajiquan user closes distance with his body initially facing forward, and his front foot stepping forward between the opponent's legs. Note that the elbow is formed with the upper arm moving to the correct height first, then the forarm retracts towards the shoulder forming the elbow shape. If the elbow were to be formed while the arm is lowered, then the resulting strike would be an uppercut instead of a thrust. In the second image post-strike, the Bajiquan user now faces sideways in Mabu (horse stance) with the elbow fully formed. This transition from a front-facing stance into the sideways Mabu is a common theme throughout Bajiquan footwork.

### Elbow Positioning
The Bajiquan elbow is not only used in a unique way, it is also positioned uniquely.  The upper arm should be just lower than parallel to the ground so as not to be pushed away during the strike. If the angle of the arm is above or below the direction of motion, the elbow will slide off the opponent rather than thrust into them. The arm is bent at the elbow with the palm and belly of the forearm facing outwards. The shoulder should be relaxed with the trapezius muscles minimally engaged. The fingers are not in a true fist shape; instead they are more akin to a loose claw, with the thumb and index finger wrapped against each other but all other fingers loosley bent. The explanation given by instructor Miyahira Tamotsu (in black below) is the loosening of the fingers allows one's mind to focus better on the tip of the elbow rather than tight forearm muscles. A wrist that is allowed to be slightly bent back further relaxes the forearm muscles and reinforces the mind-elbow proprioception.

As in the below image, the upper body is slightly caved inwards rather than expanding out at the chest. This creates a more solid structure that can retain its shape during impact as compared to keeping the arms parallel with the torso.

### Footwork
The Basic Bajiquan transition: entering from a front-facing stance and ending in a lower Mabu (horse stance) is shown below. 

Step 1: moving the front foot into the opponents space. The front foot is placed either between the opponents legs or outside, with the front foot just passed the opponent's foot. When the font leg steps, the weight remains on the back leg until the front foot is fully placed in position.
![[Pasted image 20250323141913.png]]

Step 2: grinding the front foot against the ground 90 degrees as the weight shifts to centerline, the hips drop, and the stance turns to Mabu (horse stance). As the front leg turns inwards during this transition, many Bajiquan instructors teach to use that rotational force to slam into the opponent's leg, causing it to be destabilized or at least provide a distraction for the subsequent ram attack.
![[Pasted image 20250323142359.png]]

Step 3 consists of executing the ram attack. This attack could be the elbow strike discussed above, a hip check, uppercut, etc. However, commonly these attacks are transitioned into, or melded with, throws, trips, or reaps to put the opponent on the ground.

(Images in this section obtained from: https://youtu.be/1eDDyXoUi_s?feature=shared, "This Bajiquan attack is DANGEROUS, Kevin Lee")

### Passing Guard/Deflection
To allow the ram attack to hit properly (in this case an elbow thrust), Bajiquan utilizes a two-handed deflection to simultaneously deflect an incoming strike while creating the opening for the ram attack.

This deflection uses the back hand to guard the face and throat while the front forearm strikes against the opponent's upper arm, allowing the front arm to position itself around the target area. This deflection could be done to the inside or outside of the opponent's arm depending on the intended ram attack. By using the front arm to deflect at the opponent's upper arm rather than forearm or hand, the opponent has a smaller opportunity to collapse their striking arm fast enough into a guard or block in order to stop the ram attack. The tradeoff of this defection being it's increased risk: in the time it takes for the font arm to reach the opponent's upper arm, the opponent's strike can easily land. The back hand deflection can mitigate this risk somewhat, but the Bajiquan mentiality is to keep pushing forward regardless.

### The Push and Trip
The front legs moves deep into the opponents stance, touching down the heel first before shifting weight forward. The back hand grabs the opponent's arm while the font arm elbows into the oponent's chest and wraps the front hand around the outside of the shoulder on the same side as the grabbed arm.

### Simultaneous Deflection and Striking - with Dan

Deflection with one arm followed by near-simultaneous striking or ramming is a hallmark of Bajiquan. In the below image, the Bajiquan user is deflecting a punch outwards while using the quick Dan (fundimental bouncing strike) to the opponent's head. Note that the opponent's punch has nearly landed. While this specific instance is likely due to the image being of a teaching demonstration the Bajiquan instructors that felt more practical to me, and/or were ex-military, shared the sentiment that punches *will* land. The philosophy shared by many military and bodyguard Bajiquan users is to keep pushing forward against the opponent so as to neutralize the threat against whomever is requiring protection.

### The Groin Strike
Swing the arm in an arc downward towards the groin using a cupping-like hand to strike the groin with the palm-heel. The swing serves to gain momentum with gravity helping, and to go around an  opponent's guard  at the sacrafice of initial speed.

### The Foot Trap
Often when executing strikes that are not meant to trip or throw, Bajiquan instructors teach to step on the opponent's foot to prevent them from backing away. This serves to potentially allow the initial strike to land, destabilize the opponent if they attempt to pull the affected foot back, and allow for an easier subsequent ramming attack.

### The Neck Grab and Throw
Essentially a more refined clothesline, the principle is to slip inside or over the opponents guard with one arm as the Bajiquan user takes fast steps forward. The grabbing arm waps around the opponent's neck with the crook of the elbow at the midline of the throat.

![[Pasted image 20250313191227.png]]

As the Bajiquan user passes the opponent, he puts his legs behind the opponent's legs and pushes the non-grabbing hand against the opponents back. This creates a fulcrum to help ensure the opponent is destabilzed backwards. Subsequenty the opponent is thrown to the ground.
